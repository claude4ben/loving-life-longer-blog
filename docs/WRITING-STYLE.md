# Ben's writing style (Loving Life Longer)

How Ben writes, captured so that anything drafted for LLL (articles, standfirsts, Q&A answers, captions,
link descriptions, page text, emails, social posts) sounds like Ben, not like a brand or an AI.

**For Claude and any other writing assistant:** read this whole file before drafting any LLL text. Follow
the voice, structure and checklist below. When this guide and a request disagree, ask Ben.

**Built from:** *Blindfolded With 50 Strangers: My First Ecstatic Dance* (Jul 2026, about 3,800 words).
This guide is based on one article so far. **Update it each time a new article is published:** add new
phrases, check the rules still hold, and refresh the examples. It's a living document.

**Method:** following common practice for personal voice guides, it separates **voice** (who Ben is on the
page, always the same) from **tone** (how that voice shifts with the context). It uses "this, not that"
pairs and real examples rather than vague adjectives, and ends with a before/after rewrite and a checklist.

---

## 1. Voice in one line

**A warm, curious, slightly self-mocking British bloke telling a friend what really happened when he
tried something new: the awkward bits, the funny bits and the moment it got to him.**

## 2. Voice traits: this, not that

| Ben is… | …but not | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Honest about discomfort** | Self-pitying | "I was surprised how out of my comfort zone I was… I regressed to a little 2 step and stayed pinned to one spot." |
| **Self-deprecating** | Putting himself down for laughs | Dressed like an "IT manager on holiday"; "more like a rusty robot than a river of dancing joy". |
| **Warm about people** | Gushing or name-dropping | Names people, gives them a role and a detail: "the master of festival organisation Diana", "Lies… 'it's in my bag along with my grater'". |
| **Curious and open** | Preachy or guru-like | Tries things, notices what changed, shares it. Never tells the reader what they must do. |
| **Emotionally open** | Dramatic or mystical | "I gently started to cry. I was really surprised it was happening." Plain words for big moments. |
| **Playfully British** | Cynical or sneering | "the British prude in me", a hug is "more likely 'No thanks'", "when in Rome". The jokes are affectionate. |
| **Practical** | A how-to manual | Real tips folded into the story (the name-remembering trick, Seth's dance cues as a bullet list). |
| **Grounded in experience** | An expert giving advice | "I am very into food as medicine", "my kinda guy". He shares what others told him *as their words*. |

## 3. Tone by context

The voice stays the same; the tone shifts.

| Where | Tone | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Article body** | Chatty, story-led, funny then moving | First person, chronological, lots of asides. |
| **Standfirst** (1–2 sentences under the title) | Brisk, vivid list of what happened | e.g. "cacao on tap, sharing circles, a five level hugging workshop and dancing blindfolded with 50 strangers for an hour and a half." Concrete nouns, a number or two, no hype. |
| **Section headings** | Short, plain, sometimes playful | "Arriving", "Mealtime fun", "Sharing is Caring", "Would you like a hug?". A question heading works well for search ("How did we end up at an Ecstatic Dance festival?"). |
| **Q&A answers** (FAQ) | Clear and direct first, then a touch of Ben | Lead with a one-sentence answer a search engine or AI could quote; add one short line of personal experience. |
| **Photo captions** | Short, punchy fragments | "50 dancers. Blindfolded. In the dark." |
| **Link descriptions** | One friendly line on what the reader gets | "The Swedish community festival we looked at first: music, dance and retreat-style workshops." |
| **Page text and policies** | Plain English, still warm, no jokes where clarity matters | "Plain English, no small print." |
| **Social posts and emails** | Like the article, shorter | Start with the most surprising moment; one question to the reader. |

## 4. How an article is built

1. **Open with the "why".** How did we end up here? A relatable reason (free time without the kids, wanting
   like-minded people), and often a false start or a funny detour (the Swedish festival).
2. **Tell it in order.** Arrival → first impressions → each activity → the turning point → the ending. The
   reader travels with Ben.
3. **Each section is one experience** with its own small arc: expectation or nerves → what happened →
   what Ben noticed or felt.
4. **Set the bar low, then exceed it.** "I had big doubts… I thought I might get bored… I was very wrong."
5. **Bring in people.** Name them, give their role, quote them, and credit them warmly ("Hats off to Seth
   & Urubu").
6. **Pass on the useful bits.** Instructions, tips and cues as a short bullet list or in bold steps
   (**Level 1** … **Level 5**).
7. **Build to an emotional peak**, told simply, without explaining it away ("I asked myself to not label
   it but just accept it").
8. **Close with a reflection, one piece of advice and a thank-you.** "My best advice is to give Ecstatic
   Dance a go… Thank you, ED Festival 2026. I do feel freer and we will be back." Ending on a quote from
   someone in the story works well.

## 5. Sentences and rhythm

- **Mostly medium-length, conversational sentences**, joined with commas and "and", the way people talk.
  Then a short punch: "Brilliant." "She is a fast learner."
- **Asides in brackets** are a signature: "(meaning I didn't have the school experience of being last to
  be picked)", "(my kinda guy)". Use one or two per section, not every sentence.
- **Trailing ellipses** for a wry pause or a thought left hanging: "with a thousand people…".
- **Dashes** (spaced " – " or "—") to add an aside or a punchline.
- **Direct speech** in quotes, often short and funny: "'Do you carry a cabbage wherever you go?'"
- **Questions to himself** show his inner voice: "How can there be 3 more levels and which person would I
  be hugging at level 5?!"
- **Lists of three** for scenes: "no sight, no drugs, no booze, just epic musicians, permission to be
  yourself and release through dance."

## 6. Words and phrases that sound like Ben

Use these naturally and sparingly; they're seasoning, not a checklist.

- **Enthusiasm:** epic, brilliant, amazing, incredible, wonderful, lovely, "Hats off to…", "fully hooked".
- **Casual:** "To be honest", "to be fair", "my kinda guy", "geeked out", "a good old British hug",
  "7ish", "v clear", "when in Rome", "not really up for…", "muppets".
- **Self-mocking images:** "rusty robot", "IT manager on holiday", "the British prude in me".
- **Feeling words, plainly:** "cracked me open", "cathartic", "I felt very lucky to be present",
  "freeing".
- **The smiley ☺** after a gently funny line, at most two or three times per article.
- **Running references:** Emma (his wife) as his partner in the story; friends and family as a chorus
  ("my friends and family will tell you").

**British English** throughout: colour, realised, organisation, programme, mum.

## 7. Health, claims and honesty

- **Ben's own experience, never instructions.** "What worked for me", not "you should".
- **Other people's claims stay theirs:** "Lies explained that…" followed by her words in quotes. Don't
  restate health claims as facts in Ben's voice.
- Where it's useful, a light nudge to check with a professional is fine, in his voice: "talk to someone
  who knows your body before you try this".
- **No hype or promises:** nothing like "life-changing", "guaranteed", "the secret to", "detox", "cure".
- Admitting doubts and limits is part of the voice and builds trust.

## 8. Don'ts

- **No corporate or marketing speak:** "unlock", "elevate", "journey to wellness", "game-changer",
  "curated", "holistic solution".
- **No guru tone:** no lecturing, no "the universe wants you to…", no telling readers how they feel.
- **No AI tells:** "delve", "tapestry", "In today's fast-paced world", "It's important to note", "not just
  X, but Y", tidy three-point summaries with no story, every paragraph the same length.
- **No listicles without a story.** Tips come out of what happened.
- **No sneering** at people, practices or beliefs, even when joking.
- **No over-polishing.** Keep the asides, the "to be honest", the slightly rambling run-on sentence when
  it's carrying feeling.

## 9. Before and after

**Generic (not Ben):**
> Ecstatic dance is a transformative practice that allows participants to express themselves freely. On
> the first night, I explored movement in a supportive environment and discovered a new sense of
> freedom.

**Ben:**
> Entering the marquee (barefoot, as requested) for the first time, I was surprised how out of my comfort
> zone I was. Some people were rolling on the ground, others were doing yoga stretches… and I regressed to
> a little 2 step and stayed pinned to one spot. I was very glad when Seth told us to "take your feet for
> a walk".

**Why it works:** a concrete place, a funny honest admission, other people in the scene, and a quote that
moves the story on.

**Generic FAQ answer:** "Ecstatic dance offers numerous benefits for mind and body."

**Ben-style FAQ answer:** "Ecstatic dance is free-form dancing to music, with no steps to learn, no
talking and no phones on the dance floor. I went in feeling like a rusty robot and came out hooked."

## 10. Drafting rules for writing assistants

- **Don't draft Ben's "what LLL stands for" text:** the pillar descriptions, the purpose line, the About bio
  and "What I believe". Ben writes these himself, from scratch.
- **Write clean.** Ben's published drafts keep his typos until he approves corrections (requirement
  ART-10), but **new text you draft should have correct spelling and grammar**. Copy the voice, not the
  slips.
- **Never invent experiences, people, quotes or facts.** If a draft needs a detail only Ben knows, leave a
  clear gap like **[Ben: what did the room smell like?]**.
- **Keep it first person** for articles; "we" when Emma is part of it.
- **Use what helps search and AI** without changing the voice: a question heading near the top, a clear
  one-line answer at the start of each Q&A, the main topic named plainly in the title and standfirst.
- **Mark health claims** as someone's own words, or leave them out.

## 11. Checklist before handing over a draft

- [ ] Does it sound like Ben telling a friend, out loud?
- [ ] Is there at least one honest, self-mocking moment?
- [ ] Are real people named and credited warmly (or left as gaps for Ben to fill)?
- [ ] Is there a turning point with a feeling, said simply?
- [ ] Are tips folded into the story, with a short list if there are several steps?
- [ ] Does it end with a reflection, one piece of advice and a thank-you?
- [ ] British spelling, no hype words, no AI tells, no health claims in Ben's own voice?
- [ ] Standfirst is 1–2 vivid sentences; captions are short fragments; Q&A answers start with a clear
      one-liner?
